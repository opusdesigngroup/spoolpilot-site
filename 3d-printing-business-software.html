What software does a 3D printing business actually need?

Less than the ads suggest, and different from what a normal small business uses. A small shop needs four numbers it can trust: what each print costs, what each sale actually earned after the platform took its cut, how much filament is on the shelf, and which products are worth printing again. Whether that takes a spreadsheet or an app depends mostly on how many orders a month you sell.

By Sean, who runs Opus Design Company on Etsy: five years, 3,000+ sales, a few printers in a spare room. Everything below is what that shop tracks and why. Last revised 12 September 2026.

Why a 3D printing business isn't a normal small business

Most small-business software assumes you buy finished goods, put them on a shelf, and sell them. A print shop doesn't work that way. Your inventory is raw material measured by weight, not units. Most products are made after the order arrives, so a "stock count" is often meaningless. Your machines run unattended for hours, which makes time a cost even when you aren't in the room. And a single product's cost changes when you change a print setting: a different infill, a different layer height, or a color that needs more purging all move the number.

Then there is the part nobody budgets for: prints that fail. A failed print consumes material and machine time and produces nothing. Generic bookkeeping has no line for that. A shop that doesn't count it will believe every product is more profitable than it is.

The numbers that decide whether a print made money

These are the inputs, in the order they usually go wrong. Each has a longer guide in the resources section; this page is the map.

Filament and other material

Your slicer already knows the grams for a print. Multiply by what you paid per kilogram and that is your material cost, plus supports, purge towers, and the waste from a color change if you run multi-color. The mistake is not the math. It is using a price per kilo from six months ago, or forgetting that the fancy silk PLA costs twice what the plain roll did. Material is usually the smallest cost on a small print and the largest on a big one, so a shop that sells both needs the real figure for each.

Print time

Print time matters three ways. It drives electricity. It drives wear on nozzles, belts, and build plates. And it is capacity: a six-hour print occupies a printer that could have made something else. With one printer that is easy to feel. With four, it is the difference between a product that pays for itself and one that just keeps the machines busy. The slicer reports the time; the discipline is recording it per product rather than guessing later.

Failed and unsold prints

A failure is filament and hours spent with no product to show. A prototype is the same thing on purpose. A calibration cube is a cost of doing business. If none of that is logged, your material spend will never reconcile with your sales, and you will not know which product or which printer is eating the difference. SpoolPilot logs a print as stock, prototype, or failed, and lets you log a scratch print with no product behind it, so the burned spool shows up somewhere.

Electricity and machine cost

Electricity is smaller than people fear and larger than zero. A printer that averages 150 watts over a six-hour print uses about 0.9 kWh. At the June 2026 US residential average of 18.34 cents per kWh (EIA, Electric Power Monthly, table 5.6.A), that print cost about 17 cents to run. Heated beds, enclosures, and dryers push it up; your local rate may be half or double the average. The point is to use a per-hour figure and stop rounding it to nothing.

Machine depreciation is real too: a $600 printer that gives you 3,000 print hours is 20 cents an hour before maintenance. SpoolPilot charges it per print hour as Printer wear: type what one printer cost and the print hours you expect from it in Cost settings, and every price, sale, and report carries its share. Leave it blank and nothing is charged.

Packaging

The box, the mailer, the tape, the insert, the thank-you card. Count the actual box, not a guess. On a $12 item, a $1.40 box and mailer is more than 10% of the sale, which is more than most people's electricity and material combined on that item. Packaging is the cost small shops most often leave out and most often regret.

Labor

Removing supports, sanding, inserting magnets, packing, answering the message about whether it comes in blue. Set an hourly rate even if you never pay yourself, because a product that only makes money when your time is free is not a product, it is a hobby with shipping labels. A rate of $15 to $25 an hour turns "I spent ten minutes on that" into a number you can compare across products.

Etsy fees, if you sell on Etsy

Etsy's published rates (Etsy's fee policy) are a 6.5% transaction fee on the order total including shipping, payment processing of 3% plus $0.25 in the US, a $0.20 listing fee per item sold, and an Offsite Ads fee of 12% or 15% on orders attributed to those ads. On a $24 order with $4 shipping that is roughly $1.82 + $1.09 + $0.20, about $3.11 before any ad attribution, or about 11% of what the buyer paid. What actually lands in your payment account is often more than the rate card predicts, for reasons that don't fit a formula. Why Etsy takes more than the rate card says walks through five of them and how to check your own shop in ten minutes.

If you sell at markets or through your own site instead, swap in that platform's fees or your card-processing rate. The structure is the same; only the percentages change.

Inventory by the spool

Filament inventory is the one place print shops try hardest and get the least back. Weighing every open spool is accurate and nobody keeps it up. The version that survives contact with a busy week is simpler: count sealed spools by scanning the barcode when a box arrives, treat an opened spool as "in use," and let sales pace tell you how many weeks of each material are left. That is what SpoolPilot does, and it is also a perfectly good spreadsheet column if you would rather do it by hand.

Product inventory is the other half. Most 3D-printed products are made to order, and forcing a stock count on them is a false chore. The products worth counting are the ones you print ahead of demand, which is usually your best sellers before a busy season. A print-next queue, ranked by how soon each product runs out at its current sales rate, replaces both the whiteboard and the panic.

Product-level profitability

Monthly profit tells you whether the shop is alive. Product-level profit tells you what to do next. Two items can sell equally well and one of them can be losing money on every order once packaging and labor are counted. Bundles are the usual hiding place: a three-piece set priced as a discount that costs more to print and pack than its parts sold separately. The only way to see it is to have every cost above attached to every product, and every sale's real fees attached to every order, so that "profit per product this month" is a number you can sort by rather than a feeling.

Where spreadsheets stop working

A spreadsheet is the right tool at the start, and this page is not going to pretend otherwise. It breaks in a predictable order. First, fees per order: to know the real fee on each sale you have to download the payment account CSV, match its rows to orders, and type them in, and that is the step everyone abandons by the second month. Second, variants and multiple printers: the sheet that priced one product in one color becomes forty rows that nobody trusts. Third, time: the sheet lives on a laptop, and the decisions happen at the printer, at the post office, and at the kitchen table at 11pm. By the time you sit down to update it, you are estimating, and estimates drift in one direction.

Why "on your phone" is a feature, not a convenience

A print shop is run standing up. You notice the spool is low while you are changing it. You want to know whether an order was worth it while you are packing it. You want to price a print while the slicer is open on the screen in front of you. Software that requires walking to a desk gets used on Sundays; software in your pocket gets used when the information is fresh. That is the reason SpoolPilot is an iPhone app that reads the slicer screen with the camera, rather than a web dashboard with a form.

Where SpoolPilot fits

SpoolPilot is an iPhone app for people who sell what they print. It does the four things at the top of this page and stops there. If you are choosing between it and a spreadsheet or another tool, the software decision guide is the shorter, more direct version of this section.

  • Cost per print. Point the camera at Bambu Studio, OrcaSlicer, PrusaSlicer, or Cura and it reads the print time and grams off the screen. Add your filament price, electricity, packaging, and labor rate, and you have the cost and a price that hits the margin you want after fees.
  • Real profit per order. Connect an Etsy shop (read-only) and it imports your sales, including full history, and reads the fee entries Etsy actually charged on each order directly from your payment ledger through Etsy's API, rather than estimating them from the rate card. Etsy grants that access only after reviewing an app, and SpoolPilot holds it; many tools that promise real profit are estimating. When an order's money doesn't reconcile to Etsy's total, it says so and labels the estimate.
  • Filament on the shelf. Sealed spools counted by barcode scan, weeks of stock from your real sales pace, and a low-stock nudge that only fires for materials you actually sell through.
  • Products and what to print next. Made-to-order by default, stock tracking where you want it, bundles assembled from parts, and a print-next queue ranked by how soon each product runs out.
  • Sales without a shop connection. Log a market or in-person sale in two taps and every number still works from what you enter.
  • Private by design. Your data lives on your phone and your own iCloud. There is no account and no analytics inside the app.

What it doesn't do

It runs on iPhone only; there is no Android or web version (an Android interest list is on the calculator page). Sales sync is Etsy only today; other platforms are entered by hand. It does not control printers or route jobs, does not handle tax or VAT, and does not yet count postage as a cost, so a free-shipping listing looks better than it is until you add the label. If any of those is the thing you need most, one of the tools in the table above is the better pick.
