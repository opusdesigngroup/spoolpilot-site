Etsy fees for 3D print sellers: how to calculate your real profit

On a US Etsy sale there are four fees: a transaction fee on the item and the shipping, payment processing, a listing fee, and, on orders Etsy's ads brought you, an Offsite Ads fee. To know your real profit, take what the buyer paid, subtract those fees as they appear in your payment account (not as a calculator predicts them), subtract the shipping label, and subtract what the print cost to make. This page walks through each fee, an order with shipping, and why the ledger and a calculator often disagree.

By Sean, who runs Opus Design Company on Etsy: five years, 3,000+ sales. Last revised 12 September 2026.

The four fees

The percentages below are the ones Etsy publishes for US sellers, as of the last revision of this page. Etsy changes them from time to time, so treat the numbers as an example and check Etsy's fee policy before relying on them.

Transaction fee

6.5% of the order total, and the total includes what the buyer paid for shipping and gift wrap, not just the item. This is the one 3D print sellers most often understate, because a heavy print with $6 of shipping pays the fee on the $6 too.

Payment processing

3% of the total plus $0.25 per order for US sellers, charged for taking the buyer's payment. The rate differs by country, which matters if your shop is not in the US.

Listing fee

$0.20 to list an item, charged again when it sells (the listing renews), and again if it sits unsold for four months. For a made-to-order product that sells steadily it is $0.20 per sale; for a slow one it is $0.20 every four months whether it sold or not.

Offsite Ads

Etsy advertises listings on outside platforms and charges 12% or 15% of the order, depending on the shop's revenue, on orders it attributes to those ads, with a cap per order. Small shops can opt out; above a revenue level it is mandatory. It only lands on some orders, which is exactly why estimating it is hard: a calculator either applies it to everything or to nothing, and the ledger applies it to the specific orders that came through an ad.

Buyer-paid shipping is not income

When a buyer pays $4.50 for shipping, that $4.50 shows up in your revenue and the transaction and processing fees are charged on it. Then you buy a label. If the label is $4.95, shipping was a small loss on that order; if you offer free shipping, the whole label comes out of the item price. Shipping charged to the buyer is at best a pass-through. Keep it out of "profit" and keep the label cost in "costs," and a lot of confusing months become clear.

Why estimated fee calculators differ from actual profitability

A fee calculator, including the free one on this site, applies the published rates to a price. That is the right tool for deciding what to charge. It is the wrong tool for knowing what you made, because the ledger diverges from the rate card in ways no formula captures: Offsite Ads lands on some orders and not others, coupons change the base the percentage is taken on, processing rates vary by country, regulatory operating fees appear in some regions, and refunds reverse fees partially. Why Etsy takes more than the rate card says goes through five of these with examples and a ten-minute check you can run on your own shop.

The practical rule: estimate to price, read the ledger to know.

Common mistakes

  • Applying the transaction fee to the item only and forgetting the shipping.
  • Assuming Offsite Ads never applies, or applying it to every order.
  • Counting shipping charged to the buyer as revenue you keep.
  • Pricing from the rate card and never checking the ledger, then wondering why the deposit is smaller than the spreadsheet.
  • Forgetting the listing renews on every sale and on unsold listings every four months.

Doing this by hand

If you have a handful of orders a month, open the payment account, copy the fee lines into a sheet next to each order's cost, and you have real profit. Do it at least once regardless; it is the fastest education in what Etsy actually costs. It stops being sustainable when the copying takes longer than the printing, which for most shops is somewhere around twenty or thirty orders a month. Past that, either the spreadsheet drifts back to estimates or something reads the ledger for you.
