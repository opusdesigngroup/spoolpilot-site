3D printing pricing calculator: how to price your prints

Your slicer already shows a cost for the print. That number is the filament and only the filament, at whatever price per kilogram is stored in the profile. It is missing electricity, machine wear, the prints that fail, your time, the box, and the platform's cut. Price the print from the slicer's number plus what it leaves out, solved so the margin survives the fees: price = (cost + flat fees) ÷ (1 − margin − fee%).

By Sean, who runs Opus Design Company on Etsy: five years, 3,000+ sales. Last revised 12 September 2026.

The short answer, with a worked example

Take a print the slicer prices at $1.03 of filament. Fully costed, with everything the slicer leaves out, it is $6.40 to make and pack (the sections below show where each part comes from). Say you sell on Etsy, where the percentage fees on a US sale are 6.5% transaction plus 3% processing, so 9.5% together, and the flat fees are $0.25 processing plus a $0.20 listing fee, $0.45 in all. You want to keep 40% of the price.

price = (6.40 + 0.45) ÷ (1 − 0.40 − 0.095) = 6.85 ÷ 0.505 = $13.56, so you list at $13.95 or $14.

Check it at $14: fees are 0.91 + 0.42 + 0.25 + 0.20 = $1.78, cost is $6.40, and you keep $5.82, which is 41.6% of the price. The margin held because the percentage fees were solved for rather than added afterward.

What the slicer's cost line includes

Bambu Studio, OrcaSlicer, PrusaSlicer, and Cura all show a cost for a sliced job. Out of the box it is one multiplication: the grams the job will use times the price per kilogram saved in the filament profile. Two things follow from that. First, it is a material cost, not a print cost; the hours are right there on the same screen, but nothing is charged for them unless you have set up a machine rate, which almost nobody has. Second, it is only as good as the price in the profile, and the profile usually still holds the default from when the printer was set up, or the price of a spool you bought a year ago, not the one on the printer now.

The slicer's number is a fine starting point. It is the first line of the cost, not the cost.

What the slicer's number is missing

Six things belong in the cost of a print. The slicer gives you the first one. The rest are where prints quietly lose money.

Filament

This is the slicer's line. Grams for the job, including supports and any purge or waste from a color change, multiplied by the price per kilogram you actually paid for that spool, which means checking that the profile's price is the real one. A $22 kilo makes a 47 g print cost $1.03 in material. Use the price of the roll on the printer, not the sale price you saw last month.

Print time and electricity

The slicer also tells you the hours. Electricity is your printer's average draw times the hours times your rate per kilowatt-hour. A printer averaging 150 W over a six-hour print uses 0.9 kWh; at a rate near 18 cents that is about 17 cents for the print. Enclosures, heated beds, and filament dryers push it up. It is rarely the biggest line, and it is never zero.

Machine and shop cost

A printer wears out. Divide what you paid for it, plus the nozzles, plates, and belts it will go through, by the print hours you expect from it, and you have a per-hour figure: a $600 machine over 3,000 hours is 20 cents an hour. Add a share of rent or a space you would otherwise use if that is real for you. Small shops usually fold this into a per-hour rate alongside electricity so it stops being forgotten.

Failed prints

Some prints fail. If one in twelve of a product's prints ends in the bin, every successful one has to carry a twelfth of a failure. Multiply the material and machine cost by 12 ÷ 11, about 1.09. A product with a fussy overhang might fail one in four and needs 1.33. If you have never counted your failure rate, that is the first thing to write down for a week, because it is different for every product and it is the cost people most often round to zero. The profit page covers where failures show up after the sale.

Labor

Your minutes at an hourly rate you choose. Removing supports, sanding, gluing, inserting magnets, packing, replying to the message about the color. Ten minutes at $18 an hour is $3, and on a small print that is often the largest single cost. A price that only works when your time is free is not a price, it is a subsidy.

Packaging

The box or mailer, tape, tissue, insert, and the card if you send one. Count the actual box. A $1.40 mailer on a $12 item is more than 10% of the sale.

Add those up. For the example above: the slicer's $1.03 of filament, $0.17 electricity, $1.20 machine time, a 1.09 failure multiplier on those three ($0.22), $3.00 labor, and $0.78 packaging comes to $6.40.

Why fees have to be solved for, not added

The common shortcut is to add up the cost, double it, and call the rest profit. Then the platform takes its percentage off the top and the doubled price turns out to have been a 35% margin, or a 20% one once shipping and the failure rate are in. Percentage fees are charged on the price, and the price is the thing you are trying to find, which is why they belong in the denominator of the formula rather than as a line added at the end. On a marketplace they also apply to the shipping the buyer pays, which is easy to miss: the Etsy fees page works through an order with shipping included.

Sometimes the formula returns a price the market will not pay. That is information. Either the cost has to come down (a faster print profile, cheaper packaging, less finishing), the margin has to come down, or the product should not be made. A calculator that quietly prints a price for an unreachable margin is worse than one that says so; the free one on this site tells you the highest margin that is reachable instead.

Choosing a target margin

Margin is the share of the price you keep after costs and fees; markup is the amount added on top of cost. They are different numbers and mixing them is the second most common pricing error. A 50% markup on $6.40 is a $9.60 price with a 33% margin before fees.

There is no correct margin. Pick it from two things: what you want to earn per hour of your time after the labor line has already paid you once, and what the product sells for elsewhere. If the market price leaves a thin margin, the fix is usually in the cost lines above, not in wishing.

Mistakes that make prints lose money

  • Pricing from a competitor's listing without knowing whether their number works. Their cost structure is not yours.
  • Using last season's filament price. Update the per-kilo number when a new spool goes on.
  • Leaving out labor because you enjoy it. Enjoy it and charge for it.
  • Counting buyer-paid shipping as income and forgetting the label costs money. Shipping is a pass-through at best.
  • Ignoring the failure rate. It is a multiplier on every successful print, not an occasional bad day.
  • Adding fees at the end instead of solving for them. See above.
  • Pricing a bundle as a discount without adding up its parts. Three items cost three items to print and one bigger box to ship.

Where a spreadsheet breaks

For pricing, a spreadsheet is a fine tool for a long time. One row per product, columns for the six costs and the formula, done. It stops being enough at two points. The first is variants: once a product has four sizes and six colors, the sheet grows to a page of near-duplicate rows and the filament price gets updated in some of them. The second is the retyping: every time you slice something you copy grams and hours from one screen into another, and that is the step that gets skipped, so new products get priced from memory.
